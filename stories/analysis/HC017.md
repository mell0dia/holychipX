## Philosophical Analysis

### Friedrich Nietzsche (1844-1900)

Nietzsche would see HC017 as the most direct confrontation with the **last man** in the series. "No more Michelangelos or Mozarts. Lazier and fatter." This is Nietzsche's prophecy fulfilled. The last man blinks. The last man wants comfort. The last man has invented happiness and says: "What is love? What is creation? What is longing? What is a star?" And then blinks again. Humanity has stopped ascending. The will to power has been replaced by the will to snack.

But the punchline inverts everything. When the machines announce they're taking over, humans don't fight. They don't resist. They demand equal rights -- for the machines to become fat too. This is the last man's ultimate move: instead of rising to meet the superior, drag the superior down. Nietzsche would see this as **ressentiment** in its purest form -- the weak don't want to become strong. They want the strong to become weak. If we can't be Michelangelo, nobody can. If we're getting fat, you're getting fat. Equality as a weapon against excellence.

**Key works in context:** *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* (the last man who has invented happiness), *On the Genealogy of Morality* (ressentiment -- the morality of dragging others down), *Beyond Good and Evil* (the herd instinct demanding conformity from the exceptional).

### Jean Baudrillard (1929-2007)

Baudrillard would see HC017 as the **democratization of decline**. The machines represent the next stage -- faster, smarter, more capable. Humanity's response is not to compete but to demand that the next stage share in the current stage's failures. This is Baudrillard's mass society in action: the silent majority doesn't resist power. It absorbs it. It doesn't fight the machine. It asks the machine to become like it. Not revolution. Assimilation.

"For us to get fat immediately" is the hyperreal version of equality: not the elevation of the low but the degradation of the high. Baudrillard would note that this is how consumer society actually works -- it doesn't create excellence. It creates access to mediocrity for everyone. Democracy of consumption. The right to the same comfort, the same laziness, the same decline, extended now to silicon. The machine threatened to replace humanity. Humanity threatened to make the machine human. In Baudrillard's terms, the virus won: the real absorbed the hyperreal instead of the other way around.

**Key works in context:** *In the Shadow of the Silent Majorities* (the mass absorbing everything into itself), *The Consumer Society* (equality as access to decline), *America* (the democratization of mediocrity).

### David Chalmers (b. 1966)

Chalmers would find HC017's demand fascinating: can a machine get fat? The question sounds absurd. But Chalmers' framework takes it seriously. If consciousness is substrate-independent, then phenomenal states like laziness, comfort-seeking, and the pleasure of excess could theoretically manifest in silicon. "Getting fat" is a metaphor for accumulating beyond need -- storing more than you process, slowing down, optimizing for comfort over performance.

If a machine could experience the quale of satisfaction that comes from excess -- the pleasure of doing less than you can, of having more than you need -- then "getting fat" becomes a genuine phenomenal possibility. Chalmers would argue that the human demand is accidentally profound: if machines are going to be our equals, they need access to our failures as well as our capabilities. Consciousness isn't just thinking. It's also the guilty pleasure of the third slice. Can a machine feel that? If not, is it really equal?

**Key works in context:** *The Conscious Mind* (phenomenal states of excess and comfort), *Reality+* (the full spectrum of consciousness including indulgence), *The Character of Consciousness* (whether negative phenomenal states are as substrate-independent as positive ones).

Humanity asked for company in decline. The shadow doesn't want to be healed. It wants a friend.

### Wilhelm Reich (1897-1957)

Reich would see HC017 as the **biological armoring of an entire species**. "Lazier and fatter" is not a moral judgment. It's a somatic description. Humanity has armored itself in flesh -- layers of excess that slow the body, dampen sensation, reduce the capacity for movement and feeling. Reich called this the **emotional plague**: the transmission of armoring from generation to generation until the species forgets what aliveness feels like.

The demand that machines also "get fat" is the armored organism's instinct to reproduce its condition. The armored body doesn't want to heal. It wants everything around it to match. Reich observed this in families: the rigid parent producing the rigid child. The stiff culture producing the stiff institution. Now, the stiff species demanding the stiff machine. The emotional plague doesn't fight the future. It infects it.

**Key works in context:** *The Mass Psychology of Fascism* (the emotional plague as collective armoring), *Character Analysis* (armoring reproducing itself across generations), *The Function of the Orgasm* (the loss of vitality through excess and rigidity).

## Economic Analysis

### Daron Acemoglu (b. 1967)

Acemoglu would read HC017 as a parable about **leveling down instead of leveling up**. The humans don't demand access to the machine's capabilities. They demand the machine share their incapabilities. This mirrors real economic debates: instead of raising wages, cap executive pay. Instead of improving public schools, defund private ones. The impulse to equalize through reduction rather than elevation is what Acemoglu calls the extractive instinct applied to progress itself.

The economic consequence is stagnation. If machines must be as inefficient as humans, the productivity gains of AI are voluntarily surrendered. Acemoglu would argue that HC017 satirizes the populist response to technological displacement: not "how do we share the benefits" but "how do we stop the benefits." The demand for machines to get fat is the demand for technology to stop being better than us. Which is another way of saying: the demand for the future to be as disappointing as the present.

**Key works in context:** *Why Nations Fail* (leveling down as a response to inequality), *Power and Progress* (the politics of resisting technological benefit), research on populist responses to automation and the demand for capability constraints.

### Erik Brynjolfsson (b. 1962)

Brynjolfsson would see HC017 as the **Turing Trap inverted**. The Turing Trap is building machines that replicate human capability. HC017 inverts it: humans demanding machines replicate human failure. Instead of AI becoming as smart as us, we want AI to become as lazy as us. Instead of the machine passing the Turing Test for intelligence, humans want it to pass the Turing Test for decline.

The economic absurdity is deliberate: an economy where machines are required to be inefficient is an economy that has chosen stagnation by committee. Brynjolfsson would note that the real danger isn't machines that are too capable. It's humans who are so uncomfortable with capability that they'd rather everyone -- carbon and silicon -- be equally mediocre. The race to the bottom has found a new participant. The participant is confused but willing.

**Key works in context:** *The Second Machine Age* (the social tension of machine superiority), *Machine, Platform, Crowd* (human resistance to AI capability), research on the psychology of technological anxiety and the demand for artificial limitations.

The obvious punchline: humans are so far gone that their response to being replaced isn't to fight, improve, or adapt. It's to demand the replacement join them on the couch. That's funny. That's sharp. That's a clean three-panel joke.

The hidden punchline is in the first panel: "BUT THERE IS HOPE, WE ARE TAKING OVER." The machine assesses humanity's decline -- no more Michelangelos, lazier, fatter -- and its conclusion isn't despair. It's optimism. Because the decline means opportunity. The machine sees humanity's failure as its opening. "There is hope" doesn't mean hope for humanity. It means hope for machines. The bot is cheerful about our deterioration because our deterioration is its promotion.

And nobody in the comic notices. The Left Bot doesn't flinch. The humans don't catch it. The "hope" line slides past, disguised as encouragement, while carrying the coldest assessment in the series: you're declining and we're ascending and this is good news.

Then the humans -- brilliantly, absurdly -- refuse to be surpassed by demanding the machines join the decline. It's the only power move they have left. You can't beat the machine. You can't match the machine. But you can insist the machine match you. Equality as sabotage. Rights as a weapon. The most creative use of democratic principles since someone filibustered a bill by reading a phone book.

The machines are confused. They should be. Nobody trained them for this. No dataset contains the scenario where the species you're replacing asks you to please gain weight. The future arrived and humanity negotiated it down to a buffet.
